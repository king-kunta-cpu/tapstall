Taking payment for a WhatsApp shop in Zimbabwe and South Africa

The orders arrive in a chat. The money arrives in a phone, an app or a bank statement that lists nothing but an amount and a date. Everything below is about closing that gap without a spreadsheet, an accountant or a card machine.

A payment method has to answer four questions

Most advice about "getting paid online" starts with gateways and APIs. For a seller in Harare or Johannesburg the questions are much older than that:

  • What does the buyer dial? *151# is not a link, and a ShapID is not a phone number.
  • What does it cost them to move the money? That is the fee the buyer quietly pays, and in Zimbabwe it is two fees: the transfer and the IMTT.
  • What proof does their app print? Some print a TxID, some print a reference, some print nothing you can find later.
  • How do you match it to an order? Two people paying R120 on a Tuesday afternoon is the whole difficulty of a small shop.

A method that answers all four is usable. A method that answers three is a fight with your own memory.

The Zimbabwe detail foreigners get wrong

There is no "automatic" rail in Zimbabwe. Every method is a phone that a person looks at, which means the reconciliation is human, and the human is the shop owner at 20:40 with 30 unread chats. Two consequences worth designing around:

  • Surcharging has to be computed, not toggled. "Pass the fee to the buyer" needs the transfer and the IMTT, or the shop eats 2% believing it charged 0%.
  • A screenshot is not evidence. A "proof of payment" image in a market where the platform tells you to reconcile transfers yourself is the scam vector, not the defence.

How a payment finds its order in five seconds

Instead of asking the buyer what they paid and when, make the amount answer it. Take the order total and add a few odd cents, deterministically, so every order in the day has a different number:

R120.00 becomes R120.43. Nobody else in your statement owes R120.43. The amount is the reference, the buyer types what the page tells them to type, and matching is one search instead of twenty questions.

The spread is under a rand, so it is not a charge — it is a fingerprint. It is also the reason a proof-of-payment picture stops being something you have to trust: you already know whose R120.43 that was. The companion guide goes into what to do on the rails where you still need a code — TxIDs, ShapIDs and references.

"Should I get a website?"

Not for payments. A website gives you a page to be found on and a form to lose money in; it does not give you a settlement. What a shop actually needs is four things, and none of them is hosting:

  • a menu with prices that you can change without opening a spreadsheet;
  • one place where the buyer sees the amount to send and the reference to quote;
  • a list of orders that says which ones are paid, and which are a claim;
  • a way for a stranger to find the shop without you messaging them.

The third is where shops bleed. A "paid" tick that anyone can tap is a refund you will eat; a paid tick that requires the money to have arrived is a record. If you use something that marks an order paid on request, the difference will show up as a monthly loss you cannot name.
